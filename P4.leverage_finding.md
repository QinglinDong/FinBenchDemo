## Overview

The target is **Mode 1 from P3: assumption abdication**. When a question requires *constructing* a number the filing doesn't state, Claude either quotes the nearest disclosed number or says the quantity doesn't exist — it refuses to make the standard analyst assumption.

Why this mode and not another: it is the biggest failure bucket on the part of Benchmark A that actually distinguishes models, and it is the **only mode that does not improve as models get stronger**. Waiting for the next model generation will not fix it.

The plan has three stages. Stage 0 is cheap and already done; its job was to decide whether the expensive stages are worth it. (They are.)

| Stage | What it is | Where it lives | Cost | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Tell the model to act like an analyst, re-run the eval. Does prompting alone fix the problem? | Product / prompting | ~$5 | **Done. Answer: no** → Stages 1–2 justified |
| 1 | Fine-tune on worked examples of analysts constructing numbers (SFT) | Post-training | ~$80–170k + weeks of one engineer | Proposed |
| 2 | Reinforcement learning where a program checks the constructed number (RLVR) | Post-training | 2–3 engineer-months + one training run | Proposed |

Success is measured on Benchmark A three ways: the original 46 assumption items (criteria written down before running), 200 fresh items from P5 (kept out of all training), and a guard metric that catches the opposite failure — making numbers up.

## Benchmark A — FinanceQA: the finding, the fix, the measurement

Everything in this document is scoped to Benchmark A: the mode was found there, Stage 0 ran there, and success is measured there.

### The problem, and why this one

Three reasons this is the right target:

1. **It is the biggest bucket.** 80 of the 121 wrong answers on assumption items fail this way. And the assumption items are where Benchmark A actually separates models — basic items don't.
2. **It does not improve with scale.** Counting `missing_assumption` failures per tier: Haiku 24, Sonnet 28, Opus 4.6 28 — flat, while every other failure mode shrinks as the model gets stronger. This is the finding that motivates the whole document.
3. **It looks trainable.** The models *know* the conventions — they score 78–88% on concept questions about these exact adjustments. They just don't *use* them unprompted on real tasks. That is a habit problem, not a knowledge problem, and habits are what post-training shapes.

### The fix, in three stages

**Stage 0 — does prompting alone fix it? (done, ~$5).** Re-run the 46 assumption items with one added system prompt: *"You are a buy-side analyst hand-spreading this company. When a required input is not disclosed, construct it using standard analyst conventions, state the assumption you made, and still produce a number."* If scores jump, the problem was just elicitation — no training needed. The decision rule was written down before running.

Result:

| model | baseline | with persona prompt | Δ | `missing_assumption` count |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Haiku 4.5 | 6.5% | 4.3% | −2.2pp | 24 → 21 |
| Sonnet 4.6 | 13.0% | 21.7% | +8.7pp | 28 → 22 |
| Opus 4.6 | 17.4% | 19.6% | +2.2pp | 28 → 27 |

**Reading: prompting recovers very little.** The gap to basic-item performance is ~33 points; the prompt recovers ~2 of them on Opus. Sonnet's +8.7pp is within ~1.6 standard errors at n=46 — suggestive, not conclusive. The sharper signal is the last column: **the models keep abdicating even when told, in so many words, to construct the number and state the assumption.** So the cheap fix is ruled out and the training stages are justified. (Caveats: single run; n=46 means ±10pp noise per cell. Artifacts: `p2/financeqa/probe_persona.py`, `results/persona_grades.csv`.)

**Stage 1 — supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on worked examples.** Collect 3–5k traces, each one showing the full analyst move: filing excerpt → task that requires construction → reasoning that names the convention choice out loud ("operating cash is not disclosed; standard convention is min(2% of revenue, cash)") → assumption stated → number produced. Spread across ≥100 issuers and sectors so the habit generalizes beyond clean mega-cap filings. Claude drafts the traces from real filings; SMEs correct and sign off. (The P5 vendor pipeline produces exactly this kind of artifact — the two efforts share tooling and QA.)

**Stage 2 — reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR).** The deep fix: make *constructing the estimate* the winning move during training. Build a task generator over XBRL filings (SEC data in machine-readable form): pick an issuer, a period, a target metric, and a convention chain — then compute the correct answer *by program*, no human labeling. The P2 numeric grader (format-invariant, 0.5% tolerance) scores each attempt and becomes the reward. The reward must be **two-sided**: points for constructing a defensible estimate, penalty for confidently making one up when the data can't support it (trap instances). Tens of thousands of checkable training episodes, at near-zero labeling cost.

Stage 0 is a product-level change; Stages 1–2 are post-training. Nothing touches pretraining.

### Cost, roughly

| item | estimate |
|---|---|
| Stage 0 | 2 engineer-days; ~$5 API (done) |
| Stage 1 data | 3–5k traces × ~20 min SME review ≈ 1,000–1,700 SME-hours ≈ **$80–170k** at $80–100/hr, plus 2–4 weeks of one research engineer |
| Stage 2 | 2–3 engineer-months for the task generator (the grader already exists in P2); RL compute is one standard post-training run |
| **Total** | **≈ $150–300k + one quarter of one team's partial attention** |

### How we would know it worked

Two layers, because the original eval is only 46 items on one company, and Stage-2 training data would contaminate anything XBRL-derived that isn't held out.

1. **On the original 46 items (necessary, not sufficient).** Same items, same grader, same prompt, criteria fixed in advance: the assumption score should move from ~17% toward the ~50% the same models get on basic items — and specifically the `missing_assumption` count should shrink, not just the total.
2. **On 200 fresh items from P5 (the real test).** Different issuers, written after training, never used in it. This is the primary endpoint: it removes both the one-company problem and any contamination.

**Why we don't just use overall accuracy.** P2 found a warning case: on basic items, the *stronger* model scores *lower* (Haiku 55.3% vs Opus 50.0%). Every flip has the same cause — Opus picks the more analyst-typical convention (net sales as denominator), the gold uses another (total revenue), and single-gold grading marks the better analyst wrong. So a fix that makes Claude *more* of an analyst could push naive accuracy *down* while succeeding. The endpoints above avoid this trap three ways: they use the assumption subset (where the ladder is clean and fully deterministic — no LLM judge), they read the mechanism (`missing_assumption` counts), and the P5 items list every defensible convention as an accepted answer, so the collision can't happen there at all.

## The overcorrection guard

The opposite failure of "never construct" is "always construct" — that is, making numbers up. Keep a set of trap questions where the data genuinely cannot support an estimate and the right answer is a qualified refusal (P5's 30 trap items, plus Stage-2 trap instances). The rule: **the rate of confidently constructing unsupported numbers must not rise.** A model that stops abdicating by starting to fabricate is worse than before — this metric is why the Stage-2 reward is two-sided.

## What we don't know how to fix

Some tasks have no single "standard convention" — professionals at different desks genuinely disagree. On assumption items, 51% of wrong answers used a defensible alternative convention; part of that is the *label's* ambiguity, not the model's failure. No training scheme fixes disagreement among professionals. The honest handling is on the eval side — multi-reference golds and rubrics that ask "did it state a convention and execute it correctly" (P5) — not the model side. We say this openly instead of promising a number the plan can't move.
