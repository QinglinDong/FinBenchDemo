# P3 — How Claude Fails at Finance-Practitioner Work

**Evidence base.** 444 graded responses: the 148 **Benchmark A (FinanceQA)** items, each answered by Haiku 4.5, Sonnet 4.6, and Opus 4.6 (thinking off, the paper's own prompt). Every response was graded by the P2 improved grader, which also assigns a failure category; 63 of the pairs were additionally checked by hand. Every number below traces to `p2/financeqa/results/grades.csv`; row IDs index `p2/financeqa/data/items.jsonl`. Where the hand check overturned the grader, we cite the hand verdict.

Four failure modes. The first is the one that matters: it is the biggest, it is the only one that does **not** shrink as models get stronger, and it is what the benchmark's headline number is really about.

## Overview

The organizing split is simple. **Modes 3 and 4 shrink as models get stronger** — ordinary scaling is already fixing them. **Modes 1 and 2 do not** — they are about which figure an analyst would construct or choose, not about computation. That is why Mode 1 is the P4 target and Mode 2 drives the P5 item design.

| Mode | What happens | Trend (Haiku→Sonnet→Opus 4.6) | Evidence | Feeds |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 — Assumption abdication | Won't construct a number the filing doesn't state; treats "not stated" as "doesn't exist" | **flat: 24 / 28 / 28** of 46 assumption items | 80 judged failures | P4 |
| 2 — Silent convention substitution | Computes correctly, but on a different defensible convention, without saying so | persistent at every tier | 114/208 wrong answers flagged defensible (55%) | P5 (`accepted_alternatives`, `assumption_named`) |
| 3 — Structural slips in deal math | One early wrong assumption compounds into a confident wrong MOIC/IRR | improving: 78.1 → 82.8 → 87.5% | 15 of 33 conceptual failures | — (scaling is handling it) |
| 4 — Statement-mechanics pattern-matching | Right accounting rule, wrong situation | partially improving | 7 failures + 3 hand-overturned | — (scaling; some overlap with Mode 2) |

## Benchmark A (FinanceQA) — the four modes

Each mode below gives the mechanism, the tier trend, and verbatim examples.

### Mode 1 — Assumption abdication: "if the filing doesn't state it, it doesn't exist"

**One sentence.** When the task requires constructing an estimate the document deliberately doesn't provide, Claude answers with the closest *disclosed* figure — or declares the quantity nonexistent — instead of making the standard analyst assumption.

**Mechanism.** These items are built so that the right answer requires a construction step: capitalize variable leases, add lease cost back to EBITDA, split a line item into known and unknown parts. Claude treats the filing plus formal accounting rules as the limit of what can be answered. It finds the right inputs, then either stops at the unadjusted number or cites GAAP to argue the requested quantity has no value.

Two facts make this the important mode. First, it is not a knowledge gap: the same models score 78–88% on concept questions *about these exact conventions*. They know the move; they don't make it unprompted. Second, scale doesn't touch it: `missing_assumption` failures are 24 (Haiku), 28 (Sonnet), 28 (Opus 4.6) — flat while everything else improves.

**Examples.**
- **Row 6** (Haiku): *"Compute the adjusted EBITDA Margin for 2024."* Gold 4.70%, which requires a lease-cost adjustment. Claude computes plain EBITDA/revenue = **4.53%** — a correct calculation of the thing it wasn't asked for. The "adjusted" step is silently dropped.
- **Row 53** (all three tiers): *"Estimate Variable Lease Assets in 2024."* Gold $1,502M (capitalize variable-lease cost the way the fixed-lease assets are). All three models answer **$0**; Opus gives the most fluent version: "variable payments are not included in the present value calculation … under ASC 842." True as accounting — but the question said *estimate*, and this is a refusal of that task. All three tiers fail identically.
- **Row 24** (Haiku): *"What are the statutory / marginal taxes on adjusted EBIT?"* Gold **$2,255M** — a dollar figure. Claude discusses tax *rates* at length and never produces a dollar amount. Both construction steps (build adjusted EBIT, apply the rate) are abdicated.

### Mode 2 — Silent convention substitution

**One sentence.** Claude does the calculation completely and correctly — but under a different defensible convention than the gold uses, and never mentions that a choice existed.

**Mechanism.** Most finance metrics have convention families: revenue vs net sales; cash vs cash-plus-short-term-investments; federal-only vs combined tax rate. Claude picks one — usually the most literal reading of the statement line — and never flags the fork. Unlike Mode 1, the work *is* done; it's done on a different base. This is why 55% of all wrong answers (114/208) carry the grader's "defensible alternative" flag.

The sting: the choice Claude makes is often the *more* professional one. P2 found that every basic item where Haiku beats Opus is this mode — Opus margins on net sales (the analyst-typical choice for a retailer), the gold uses total revenue, and single-gold grading marks the better analyst wrong. Capability *increases* the collision rate.

*Boundary note:* Row 53 above involves a convention too (ASC 842). It sits in Mode 1 because the model declined the construction itself; when the work is complete, the case lands here.

**Examples.**
- **Rows 5, 80, 82** (Opus): EBITDA margin, asset turnover, and sales-per-sq-ft all computed on **net sales ($249,625M)** where the gold uses **total revenue ($254,453M)** — three "wrong" answers from one unflagged denominator choice. On row 5 Sonnet even *writes down* the choice ("Note: Using Net Sales rather than Total Revenue") — and still takes the non-gold branch.
- **Row 33** (Haiku): *"How much cash and cash equivalents did Costco have at year end 2024?"* Gold $11,144M — the analyst convention adds short-term investments. Claude answers **$9,906M**, the literal balance-sheet line. The miss then propagates into every downstream item built on excess cash.
- **Row 23** (Haiku): *"What is the marginal tax rate in 2024?"* Gold 24.00% (21% federal + 3% state). Claude answers **21.0%** — and even lists the 3% state line in its own breakdown without adding it in.

### Mode 3 — Structural slips in multi-step deal math

**One sentence.** In LBO/M&A mini-models, Claude sets one structural assumption wrong early — debt paydown, share count, a price ladder — executes everything after it flawlessly, and reports a confidently wrong headline number.

**Mechanism.** These problems require holding a 4–8 step deal structure in working memory. The arithmetic inside each step is essentially always right; the failure is one mis-set term whose error compounds through to MOIC/IRR. This mode improves sharply with tier (78.1% → 82.8% → 87.5%), so it behaves like a capability limit that scaling and longer thinking will keep eating. P4 does not target it.

**Examples.**
- **Row 119** (Haiku): gadget-LBO problem, gold **20 gadgets**. Haiku mis-derives the year-1 price ($2.50 instead of $10) and lands on **43**. Opus walks the identical ladder correctly to 20.
- **Row 103** (Sonnet and Opus): LBO with EBITDA growing 200M→300M, gold **3x MOIC, 25% IRR**. Both models assume the $400M entry debt is fully repaid by exit and report **3.75x / 30.2%**. One assumption flips the answer; everything downstream is internally consistent.
- **Row 111** (Haiku): five-year hold, gold **2x MOIC, 15% IRR**. Haiku builds the whole model but mis-sets exit debt: **1.71x / 11.6%**.

### Mode 4 — Statement-mechanics pattern-matching

**One sentence.** On three-statement-flow questions, Claude reaches for a memorized rule ("financing doesn't change EV", "non-cash transaction") and applies it where it doesn't govern.

**Mechanism.** These questions have well-known textbook answers, and the models appear to match the surface pattern instead of tracing the actual transaction. The tell: the recited principle is correct *in general* and wrong *here*. An acquisition funded by cash and debt does change EV — an operating asset was added. Inventory bought with debt does hit the cash-flow statement. Partially tier-sensitive (Opus gets row 99 right; the others don't). Distinct from Mode 3 — there is no compounding; the error is one qualitative direction call at step one. Where this mode blurs into Mode 2, the hand-check notes say so (`human_labels.csv`).

**Examples.**
- **Row 99** (Haiku and Sonnet; hand-verified failures): firm buys a $60M business with $30M new debt + $30M cash. Gold: **EV +60M**. Haiku: "Enterprise Value is **unaffected** … the financing method doesn't affect EV" — the right rule for a *refinancing*, misapplied to an *acquisition*. Sonnet double-counts to **+90M**. (The grader passed both via the Stage-1 false-accept documented in P2; the hand labels govern.)
- **Row 142** (Sonnet): $300 of inventory bought by issuing debt. Gold: CFO −300, financing +300, net 0. Claude: "**No impact** (non-cash transaction)" — collapsing two real, offsetting flows into a rule about barter.
- **Row 137** (Haiku): $100 write-down at 20% tax. Gold: net income −80, **cash +20** (the add-back exceeds the income hit). Claude concludes "net cash impact **−$80**" — sign-flipping the consequence its own line items imply.

## What the taxonomy says as a whole

Modes 3 and 4 are being fixed by ordinary capability growth. Modes 1 and 2 are not — they are about *which figure an analyst would construct or choose*, and together they account for the assumption-question cliff (17.4% even for Opus 4.6) plus most of the defensible-alternative mass. So Mode 1 is the highest-leverage training target (P4), and multi-reference golds plus named assumptions are the top design requirement for extending the eval (P5).
